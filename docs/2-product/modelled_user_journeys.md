# Modelled User Journeys — Hobbi

*Created 1 Sep 2026 to expose and test the assumptions behind the Hobbi prototype.*

> **Evidence status:** These are representative journeys modelled from the project's desk research and product hypotheses. They are not participant interviews or primary research, and they do not complete OW-01. Lines in quotation marks are illustrative user voice, not verbatim participant testimony.

## T1 — 14-year-old, Secondary 2

Interested in crochet after seeing short videos online. She found tutorials easily but could not find a nearby beginner group with people her age. She was uncomfortable attending an adult-oriented workshop alone. Her mother wanted to know the venue, organiser and finishing time.

Illustrative user voice:

- “I want to meet friends outside of school.”
- “I don’t want to be the only young person there.”
- “My mum would ask who is running it.”

**Main barrier:** Social uncertainty and missing age information.

**Hobbi implication:** Show participant age, newcomer friendliness, exact session details and organiser verification.

## T2 — 16-year-old, Secondary 4

Plays casual futsal when invited by friends. He does not actively search for organised activities because his school and friend group already provide enough options. Parental involvement is minimal for free activities nearby.

Illustrative user voice:

- “If my friends are going, I’ll probably go.”
- “I wouldn’t download an app just to find futsal.”
- “The main problem is finding time.”

**Main barrier:** School workload and scheduling, not discovery or adult coordination.

**Hobbi implication:** This weakens the universal discovery claim. Hobbi is more relevant when a teen lacks an existing social route into an activity.

## T3 — 13-year-old, Secondary 1

Wanted to try coding or robotics but found a mixture of holiday camps, expensive courses and outdated event pages. He asked a parent for help comparing them. The parent postponed the decision because prices and commitments were unclear.

Illustrative user voice:

- “Everything looked like a full course.”
- “I just wanted to try it once.”
- “My dad said we would check later.”

**Main barrier:** Difficulty finding a low-commitment first session and coordinating a decision with a busy adult.

**Hobbi implication:** Lead with inexpensive, reversible trials and provide a clear parent summary.

## T4 — 15-year-old, Secondary 3

Tried a dance session and did not return. She enjoyed dancing but felt the other participants already knew one another. She would consider another studio or a session designed for newcomers.

Illustrative user voice:

- “The dancing was fun; the group was awkward.”
- “Everyone already had their own friends.”
- “I’d try again somewhere else.”

**Main barrier:** The specific social environment, not the activity.

**Hobbi implication:** Do not convert one negative session into “dislikes dance.” Record whether the problem was the activity, instructor, group or environment.

## C1 — Caregiver of a 14-year-old

Generally supports free or inexpensive activities but wants to know who operates them, where the child will be, how they will travel home and whether payment creates a longer commitment. Being asked for these details across several messages makes approval slower.

Illustrative user voice:

- “Free doesn’t always mean there are no extra costs.”
- “Tell me who is responsible.”
- “I don’t want to approve the same plan five times.”

**Main barrier:** Information is fragmented between the teen, provider and caregiver.

**Hobbi implication:** Give the caregiver one concise approval view containing organiser, venue, timing, transport, total cost and what changed.

## Patterns exposed by the journeys

These journeys expose three product hypotheses worth testing:

1. Teens without an existing friend or school pathway may struggle to enter an activity alone.
2. The social environment may determine whether a teen returns more than the activity category itself.
3. Adult coordination may delay attendance when safety, transport, payment or commitment information is incomplete.

One scenario deliberately challenges Hobbi: some teens already discover activities successfully through friends and mainly struggle with time. The product should therefore not claim that every teenager has a discovery problem.

## How to use this artifact

These journeys may be used to:

- test product flows and modelled personas;
- identify assumptions that need validation; and
- explain which hypotheses informed the prototype.

They must not be described as completed interviews, participant testimony or evidence that the hypotheses are true. Suitable deck wording is:

> “We modelled five representative discovery journeys and identified three hypotheses for field validation.”

If no real interviews are conducted, the adult-coordination and “scene, not class” observations remain hypotheses, and OW-01 remains open or is explicitly dropped.
