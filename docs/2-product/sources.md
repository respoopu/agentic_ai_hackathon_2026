## 0. What the verification pass changed

On 27 Aug 2026 every load-bearing claim was chased to a primary document. Four outcomes worth knowing about:

### ⛔ One headline figure was retracted

*"Only 42% of youths are satisfied with the opportunities available to pursue a hobby — NCSS Quality of Life Survey 2025."* **Does not check out.** Details in §A.2. It was one of two numbers we planned to open with.

### ✏️ One citation had the wrong authors

The left/right-brain paper was held as *"Lim, S. W. H. & Bonga, M."* The journal, volume, pages, year and DOI were all correct; **the author names were wrong.** It is **Shin, D. D., Lee, M., & Bong, M.** See §F. A judge with a psychology background is exactly the person most likely to look this one up.

### ⬆️ Three arguments got materially stronger

- The **CCA objection** now cites MOE directly instead of Wikipedia — and MOE's actual wording (CCA is graded and converts to admissions bonus points) makes the argument sharper than the version it replaces. §E.
- Three claims that were flagged in v1 as *"my general knowledge, no retrieved source"* now have peer-reviewed citations: interest development, maturation and talent ID, and habit formation. §I.
- The **IMDA framework** section was quoted from the actual 51-page PDF rather than law-firm summaries, which corrected both the version story and the "effective date" framing. §G.

### 🧭 The methodological lesson, which is the important one

The 42% figure survived in our brief because it appeared in a credible news article and nobody opened the primary source. The research pass that caught it also found that **automated page-summarisers returned inconsistent and occasionally fabricated quotes for the same URL** — including confidently reporting that text was absent from a page where it was present verbatim.

> **An AI summary of a source is a lead, not a citation.** Anything going on a slide gets opened, read, and quoted from the actual document.

That rule applies to this file too. Every 🟢 below was read directly.

## F. Neuromyths (left/right brain)

### ✏️ Citation correction

🟢 **Shin, D. D., Lee, M., & Bong, M. (2022). "Beyond left and right: Learning is a whole-brain process." *Theory Into Practice*, 61(3), 347–357.** DOI: 10.1080/00405841.2022.2096386
https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00405841.2022.2096386

**v1 held this as "Lim, S. W. H. & Bonga, M." — wrong.** Journal, volume, issue, pages, year and DOI were all correct; the author names were not. Verified via Crossref, Semantic Scholar and ERIC. The real authors are Dajung Diane Shin, Minhye Lee and Mimi Bong (Korea University, Brain & Motivation Research Institute). "Bonga" is a corruption of "Bong".

The paper's own opening line, confirmed: *"Are there really 'right-brained' and 'left-brained' learners? The argument of left- and right-brain learning is the **second most pervasive neuromyth in education**."*

**Still our strongest single citation for the constraint. Just cite it correctly.**

### The supporting evidence

🟢 **Macdonald, K., Germine, L., Anderson, A., Christodoulou, J., & McGrath, L. M. (2017). "Dispelling the Myth: Training in Education or Neuroscience Decreases but Does Not Eliminate Beliefs in Neuromyths." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 8, 1314.** DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01314 · PMC5554523

Exact item and data confirmed. The statement *"Some of us are 'left-brained' and some are 'right-brained', and this helps explain differences in learning"* (correct answer: FALSE) was endorsed by **64% of the general public, 49% of educators, and 32% of the high-neuroscience-exposure group.** Use these three numbers rather than a vague "neuromyth" reference — a precise figure is what the deck asks for.

🟢 **Nielsen, J. A., Zielinski, B. A., Ferguson, M. A., Lainhart, J. E., & Anderson, J. S. (2013). "An Evaluation of the Left-Brain vs. Right-Brain Hypothesis with Resting State Functional Connectivity Magnetic Resonance Imaging." *PLoS ONE*, 8(8), e71275.** DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0071275

Resting-state fMRI, **1,011 individuals aged 7–29, 7,266 brain regions**. Finding: *"lateralization of brain connections appears to be a local rather than global property"* — nobody has an overall dominant hemisphere. **This is the imaging study the debunking rests on. Use it in place of the Forbes article.**

🟡 **APA, *Monitor on Psychology*, "No such thing as 'right-brained' or 'left-brained'"** — Vol 44 No 10, Nov 2013. https://www.apa.org/monitor/2013/11/right-brained
The APA-native secondary summary of Nielsen et al. *(Existence and framing confirmed via indexing; direct fetch was blocked, so treat the framing as 🟡.)*

⚠️ **There is no formal APA position statement on left/right-brain typing.** v1 implied one via a Forbes article. The 2019 APA press release *"Belief in Learning Styles Myth May Be Detrimental"* is about **VAK learning styles generally** and does not mention left/right-brain — do not cite it for this claim.

⛔ **Forbes (Boser, 2023)** — reported the APA position second-hand. **Superseded by Nielsen et al. Dropped.**

## G. IMDA Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI

*v1 cited law-firm summaries. Replaced with the framework PDF itself, which corrected two things.*

### ✏️ Two corrections

1. **There are two versions, not one document with two dates.**
   - **v1.0** — announced **22 Jan 2026** at WEF Davos by Minister Josephine Teo.
   - **v1.5** — published **20 May 2026**, further updated 5 June 2026, incorporating feedback from 60+ organisations and adding case studies (AWS, DBS, Google, Workday, OCBC, Tencent, PwC, GovTech) plus expanded coverage of multi-agent systems, third-party agents and automation bias.
2. **"Effective 20 May 2026" is the wrong framing.** It is a **voluntary Model Framework** and a *"living document"*, not legislation. Say **"aligned with"**, never "compliant with". A Singaporean judge will know the difference.

### Primary sources

🟢 **Framework PDF (v1.5)** — https://www.imda.gov.sg/-/media/imda/files/about/emerging-tech-and-research/artificial-intelligence/mgf-for-agentic-ai.pdf
🟢 v1.0 press release — https://www.imda.gov.sg/resources/press-releases-factsheets-and-speeches/press-releases/2026/new-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai
🟢 v1.5 announcement — https://www.imda.gov.sg/resources/press-releases-factsheets-and-speeches/updated-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai

### The four dimensions, quoted

1. **Assess and bound the risks upfront** — *"understanding the risks posed by the agent's actions, which depend on factors such as the scope of actions the agent can take, the reversibility of those actions, and the agent's level of autonomy… limit their agents' scope of impact by designing appropriate boundaries at the planning stage, such as limiting access to tools and external systems."*
2. **Make humans meaningfully accountable** — *"'human-in-the-loop' has to be adapted to address automation bias… This includes defining significant checkpoints in the agentic workflow that require human approval, such as high-stakes or irreversible actions, and regularly auditing human oversight to check that it remains effective over time."*
3. **Implement technical controls and processes** — testing for *"overall execution accuracy, policy adherence, and tool use."*
4. **Enable end-user responsibility** — *"users should be informed of the agent's range of actions, access to data, and the user's own responsibilities."*

### The risk-factor table (framework §2.1) — this is the slide

- *"Agent's access to sensitive data… the risk increases if the agent has **persistent memory** and can store sensitive data across sessions."*
- *"Scope of agent's actions — whether an agent can only read from or can also modify the data and systems it has access to… **Read vs write**."*
- *"**Reversibility** of agent's actions — if the agent can modify data and systems, whether such modifications are easily reversed… Modifications may not be easily reversed if they trigger downstream obligations e.g. entering into a contract or sale."*
- *"Agent's **level of autonomy** — whether the agent can define the entire workflow or must follow a well-defined procedure."*

### Approval checkpoints (framework §2.2)

> *"Organisations should define significant checkpoints or action boundaries that require human approval… **High-stakes actions**…; **Irreversible actions e.g. permanently deleting data, sending communications, making payments**; Outlier or atypical behaviour…"*

**"Making payments" and "sending communications" are named explicitly.** Our Broker does both, for a minor. Quote this line on the governance slide.

### Tool restriction and sandboxing

> *"Apply the principle of least privilege to limit tools available to each agent… Whitelist trusted servers…; **Sandbox any code execution**."*
> *"Authorisations should generally be scoped, time- or session-bound, non-transferable, and follow the principle of least privilege by default, with explicit escalation paths for elevated permissions."*

🟡 Law-firm summaries — Bird & Bird, K&L Gates, Stephenson Harwood, Mayer Brown, Regulations.ai. Retained for context only; the PDF supersedes all of them.

## G2. PDPA and minors' data — new in v2

🟢 **PDPC, *Advisory Guidelines on the PDPA for Children's Personal Data in the Digital Environment*, issued 28 March 2024.**
https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/advisory-guidelines/advisory-guidelines-on-the-pdpa-for-children's-personal-data-in-the-digital-environment_mar24.pdf

Directly load-bearing because our users are 13–17. The PoC is text-only; the voice-recording guidance governs any future audio adapter.

- *"'child' or 'children' refers to an individual or individuals who is or are **below 18 years of age**."*
- > *"The PDPC considers that a **child between 13 and 17 may give valid consent**, when the policies on the collection, use and disclosure of the child's personal data, as well as the withdrawal of consent, are **readily understandable by them**. This includes ensuring that the child understand the consequences of providing and withdrawing consent. However, where an organisation has reason to believe that a child does not have sufficient understanding… the organisation should obtain consent from the child's parent or guardian."*
- *"Where the child is **below 13** years of age, the organisation **must** obtain consent from the child's parent or guardian."*
- *"The personal data of children is generally considered to be **sensitive personal data** and must be accorded a higher standard of protection under the PDPA."* — citing *Singapore Taekwondo Federation* [2018] SGPDPC 17.
- Consent obtained while a child *"remains valid when the individual reaches 18 years of age."*
- PDPC explicitly contemplates organisations setting a **higher** bar in context, giving an education setting as its example.

**Design consequence** (see [`architecture.md`](../3-system/architecture.md) §8): the teen can self-consent for preference and attendance data if our consent copy is genuinely readable by a 13-year-old — so that copy is a deliverable, not boilerplate. A future voice adapter additionally requires teen **plus** parent consent, a named processor and a deletion test.

## I. Claims that had no source in v1 — now resolved

*v1 listed eight claims as "my general knowledge or my own analysis." Three now have peer-reviewed citations, two are honestly downgraded, and three remain our own analysis and are labelled as such.*

### ✅ I.1 — "Interest gets developed, not diagnosed" — **now sourced, and it is the intellectual spine of the product**

🟢 **Hidi, S., & Renninger, K. A. (2006). "The Four-Phase Model of Interest Development." *Educational Psychologist*, 41(2), 111–127.** DOI: 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4

Four phases: **triggered situational interest → maintained situational interest → emerging individual interest → well-developed individual interest.** Described in the citation literature as the most frequently used theoretical underpinning of interest research.

🟢 **Hidi, S. E., & Renninger, K. A. (2025). "The four-phase model of interest development: Addressing individual differences." *Learning and Individual Differences*.** DOI: 10.1016/j.lindif.2025.102865 — the original authors updating their own model. *(In press / early online; the formal year may resolve to 2026.)*

🟢 **Renninger, K. A., & Hidi, S. (2016). *The Power of Interest for Motivation and Engagement*. Routledge.** — frames interest as something *"all persons, regardless of age or context, can be supported to develop."*

🟢 **Yan, Y., Jia, Y., Li, Y., Liu, F., & Zhang, M. (2025). "From situational interest and state curiosity to personal interest." *Frontiers in Psychology*.** DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1700208
> *"interests solidify through an accumulation of positive experiences… or are altered through an accumulation of negative experiences"*, with *"reengagement in specific activities"* as a key mechanism. Also notes situational interest in childhood is triggered more by emotional factors and in adolescence more by cognitive ones — directly relevant to a 13–17 product.

**Why this matters more than the other citations.** It converts our central design decision from a preference into a finding: an assessment cannot measure a phase-4 interest in someone who has not had a phase-1 trigger, so the only way forward is to arrange triggers and observe. That *is* a portfolio of cheap experiments. See `project_brief.md` §3.2.

### ✅ I.2 — Maturation confounds adolescent talent ID — **now sourced**

🟢 **Malina, R. M., Rogol, A. D., Cumming, S. P., Coelho-e-Silva, M. J., & Figueiredo, A. J. (2015). "Biological maturation of youth athletes: assessment and implications." *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 49(13), 852–859.** DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2015-094623
→ Selection and exclusion in youth sport *"follows a maturity-related gradient largely during the interval of puberty and the growth spurt."* Also flags validity problems with the anthropometric maturity-estimation equations used in the field.

🟢 **Cumming, S. P., Lloyd, R. S., Oliver, J. L., Eisenmann, J. C., & Malina, R. M. (2017). "Bio-banding in Sport." *Strength & Conditioning Journal*, 39(2), 34–47.**
→ The founding bio-banding review. Grouping by maturation rather than age exists *because* age-matched children vary enormously in biological maturity.

🟢 **Cumming, S. P., et al. (2018). "Biological maturation, relative age and self-regulation… A test of the underdog hypothesis." *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*, 39, 147–153.**
→ The mechanism for reversal: later-maturing players must develop superior technical, tactical and psychological skills to survive selection, and those advantages *"become more salient in late adolescence and early adulthood when age and/or physical maturity are attenuated or reversed."*

🟡 **Ostojic, S. M., et al. (2014). "The biological age of 14-year-old boys and success in adult soccer." *Research in Sports Medicine*, 22(4), 398–407.** PubMed 25295477
→ Reported finding: among players who reached the top five European leagues, all had been in the late-maturing group at 14. **The paper is confirmed; that specific figure comes from a secondary summary, not a fetched primary quote.** Verify before putting the number on a slide.

### ⚠️ I.3 — Body-composition feedback harms adolescents — **partially sourced, and be honest about the gap**

🟢 **Levinson, J. A., Kinkel-Ram, S., Myers, B., & Hunger, J. M. (2024). "A systematic review of weight stigma and disordered eating cognitions and behaviors." *Body Image*, 48, 101678.** DOI: 10.1016/j.bodyim.2023.101678 · PMC11180546
→ Weight stigma described as *"one central factor in the pathogenesis of eating disorders"*, across binge eating, dietary restraint, unhealthy weight control, drive for thinness and body dissatisfaction.

🟢 **Adolescent weight-teasing literature** — Neumark-Sztainer's original work (PMID 11791157); *"Associations Between Weight-Based Teasing and Disordered Eating Behaviors Among Youth"* (2021, *Eating Behaviors*, PMC8131258): 201 youth aged 8–17; weight-based teasing associated with emotional eating, eating in the absence of hunger, and a **threefold greater likelihood of a recent loss-of-control eating episode**. A 15-year longitudinal study found adolescent weight teasing predicted unhealthy eating and higher BMI up to 15 years later.

🟡 **Webb, M. D., et al. (2024). "Associations between anthropometry, body composition, and body image in athletes: a systematic review." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 15.** DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1372331
→ Higher BMI / % body fat correlated with greater body dissatisfaction in **16 of 22 studies (72.7%)** — but the underlying studies are **cross-sectional**.

> **The honest position.** No study isolates *"an app gives a 13–17-year-old their body-composition number"* as the exposure. The mechanism is inferred by analogy from a strong adjacent literature on weight stigma and weight-related commentary. **Say it that way if pressed** — and note that the design decision stands on caution and equity grounds regardless of whether the causal chain is proven. That is a better answer than overclaiming.

### ✅ I.4 — Habit formation and peer adherence — **new in v2, and it disciplines what we promise**

🟢 **Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world." *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 40(6), 998–1009.** DOI: 10.1002/ejsp.674
→ 96 volunteers, 12 weeks. Time to reach **95% of an individual's automaticity plateau ranged from 18 to 254 days.** **Cite the range, not "66 days"** — that is a median from this same study, not a law, and quoting it is a tell that the paper was not read.

🟢 **Lin, H., Chen, H., Liu, Q., Xu, J., & Li, S. (2024). "A meta-analysis of the relationship between social support and physical activity in adolescents." *Frontiers in Psychology*.** DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1305425
→ 56 studies, 65 samples, **N = 47,196**. **Peer support r = 0.256** with adolescent physical activity (family 0.226, general social support 0.298), partially mediated by self-efficacy. Directly supports the belonging objective.

🟢 **Crane, J., & Temple, V. (2015). "A systematic review of dropout from organized sport among children and youth." *European Physical Education Review*, 21(1), 114–131.** DOI: 10.1177/1356336X14555294
→ 43 studies. Five dropout drivers: lack of enjoyment, perceptions of competence, **social pressures**, competing priorities, physical factors. Interpersonal constraints appear more often than structural ones.

⛔ **"70% of kids quit sport by 13"** — widely circulated, contested and unattributed. **Do not use.** Cite Crane & Temple instead.
